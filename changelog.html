Chapter 3: Convergence and The Basics of Inference

New

  • Solutions to all four self-test problems.

Chapter 4: Nonparametric Estimation and The Bootstrap

Corrected

  • Comparing Bootstrap Confidence Intervals: the transformation property was attributed to the wrong interval. The percentile interval respects increasing transformations, because quantiles commute with increasing maps. The pivotal interval does not.
  • Comparing Bootstrap Confidence Intervals and the Quick Self-Check: removed the claim that the pivotal interval is often the most accurate of the three. Its coverage can fall short. A new simulation shows this for the median.

New

  • Confidence Sets: The Foundation: confidence sets in general, before intervals. A note covers other shapes: bands, regions, unions of intervals.
  • The Core Idea of the bootstrap: Intuitive, Mathematical and Computational tabs. New figure of the real world versus the bootstrap world.
  • Worked examples with solutions: “The plug-in principle by hand” and “The bootstrap without Monte Carlo” (all 27 resamples of three observations).
  • Simulation examples: “100 Confidence Intervals” (what 95% coverage means) and “Do the three intervals actually cover 95% of the time?” (the three bootstrap intervals for the median).
  • Solutions to all four self-test problems.

Revised

  • Self-test problems 2 (Correlation CI) and 3 (Ratio Statistics) ask more specific questions. Numbering unchanged.

Chapter 5: Parametric Inference I: Finding Estimators

Corrected

  • Properties of Method of Moments Estimator: the asymptotic covariance was given as g\,\mathbb{E}_\theta(YY^T)\,g^T, as in All of Statistics. That holds only when Y has mean zero. The correct form is g\,\mathrm{Cov}_\theta(Y)\,g^T.
  • FAQ: Common Issues in Numerical Optimization: a local optimiser does not always find the nearest valley. It finds a local minimum, usually the nearest one. The demonstration in the same section shows an exception.

New

  • Interactive demo “The Moment-Matching Game” (MoM in Action: Examples; HTML only). Fit a Gamma or Beta model by hand by matching its mean and variance to the data.
  • Figure of a single Newton step with its quadratic approximation.
  • Constrained optimisation: hard bounds (what the optimiser must respect) versus plausible bounds (where starting points are drawn from).
  • Solutions to all four self-test problems.

Revised

  • Which Optimizer Should I Use?: organised by what you can evaluate (exact objective and gradient, noisy estimates only, no gradients) instead of by dataset size. L-BFGS is recommended whenever the exact gradient is available, however large the dataset.
  • FAQ: Common Issues in Numerical Optimization: always run the optimiser from several starting points. New explanation of why one demo run ends in the far valley.
  • Self-test problems 1 and 2 replaced. Problem 1: Method of Moments for Uniform(-\theta, \theta) (was Uniform(a, b)). Problem 2: maximum likelihood for Geometric(p) (was Poisson(\lambda)). Numbering unchanged.

Chapter 6: Parametric Inference II: Properties of Estimators

Corrected

  • Fisher Information (Computational tab): the bottom panel of the Bernoulli figure plotted the same log-likelihood curve three times, under labels for three values of p. It now shows three different datasets: 10, 12 and 16 successes out of 20.
  • Self-test problem 3 (Delta Method): the estimator was written \hat{\tau} = \log(p/(1-p)), a function of the unknown p. It is \hat{\tau} = \log(\hat{p}/(1-\hat{p})).

New

  • Interactive demo “EM Step by Step” (The Expectation-Maximization (EM) Algorithm; HTML only). Pick the starting means of a two-component Gaussian mixture, then step through the iterations.
  • Solutions to all five self-test problems.

Revised

  • EM Computational tab: the 2D three-component example is replaced by a full implementation for the 1D two-component mixture used in the rest of the section.
  • Self-test problem 2 (Confidence Intervals) replaced: a Poisson error-rate problem instead of the coin-flip one. Numbering unchanged.

Chapter 7: Hypothesis Testing and p-values

Corrected

  • Two Ways to Be Wrong: Type I and Type II Errors: \beta stood both for the power function and for the probability of a Type II error, so the power was written as 1 - \beta. The power is \beta(\theta), and the probability of a Type II error is 1 - \beta(\theta).
  • Two Ways to Be Wrong: Type I and Type II Errors: the power was defined as a single probability. Unless H_1 is simple, the power depends on the true value of the parameter.

Revised

  • The “Adjusted p-values” box (was “Adjusted p-values: An Alternative Presentation”) is rewritten. It starts from the definition. Bonferroni adjusted p-values are added alongside Benjamini–Hochberg. The example now shows the monotonicity step lowering a value.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg figure: now includes a p-value above its own threshold that is still rejected. An inset shows why.

Chapter 8: Bayesian Inference and Statistical Decision Theory

Minor edits only.

Chapter 9: Linear and Logistic Regression

Corrected

  • Checking Assumptions: Residual Plots: the errors were written as \epsilon_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i. That is the definition of the residuals \hat{\epsilon}_i. The errors themselves are unobserved.
  • Footnote in Estimating Parameters: The Method of Least Squares: ridge regression does keep a closed-form solution. Only a LASSO penalty or constraints on the coefficients lose it.

New

  • Section “Categorical Predictors with More Than Two Levels”: treatment (dummy) coding, testing a factor as a whole. Worked example: “Age groups in the Framingham data”.
  • Worked examples: “Age as a factor or as a single number” (AIC versus BIC) and “Age group in the logistic model” (likelihood ratio test for a factor).
  • Self-test problem 6, “Changing the Reference Level”, with solution.
  • Simple Linear Regression in Practice: the Framingham data file can be downloaded, and its source is given.

Revised

  • LIME removed. Linear Models as Building Blocks and The Big Picture now use linear probes of a large language model. New figure: the world map a probe reads out of the model’s activations.
  • Coefficients are interpreted as associations, not effects. For example, one unit higher relative weight “is associated with” about 0.5 mmHg higher systolic blood pressure (was: “increases”).
  • Adding Multiple Predictors: the odds-ratio interpretation is now an exercise, “Reading the odds ratios”, with solution.

Chapter 10: Practical Bayesian Inference via Probabilistic Programming

Corrected

  • The az.compare() table and self-test problem 4 (Interpreting ELPD-LOO): elpd_diff was described as negative for the worse models. ArviZ reports zero for the best model and positive values for the others. Text, decision rule and problem fixed.
  • Convergence diagnostics: several passages said that \hat{R} \approx 1 and agreeing chains confirm convergence. They cannot. They can only reveal a failure to converge, because all the chains can miss the same region of the posterior.
  • From Continuous to Categorical: Medical Discretization: the Framingham data were said to be from the 1990s. They are from the 1940s–1960s. Claim removed.
  • The old comparison of three priors concluded from non-significant LOO differences that the top prior was validated. It also concluded that the results were robust to the prior. Neither follows. Replaced by a prior sensitivity analysis: with 30 observations, the prior visibly changes the posterior.
  • Stan code: the old array syntax real y[n] is replaced by array[n] real y. Current versions of Stan reject the old syntax.

New

  • Worked example with solution: “The A/B Test From Chapter 7, the Bayesian Way”. Chapter 7’s A/B test in PyMC, checked against the exact Beta posteriors.
  • Figures on the probability simplex: Dirichlet distributions, and the prior and posterior of the cholesterol example.
  • Exact conjugate check of the MCMC results.
  • Estimating the Parameters of a Normal Distribution: link to download the Framingham data file.
  • Optional sections: “Faster Sampling With JAX and Numba”, “Finnish Terminology Reference”.

Revised

  • “Comparing Priors With Model Comparison” replaced by “Does Cholesterol Differ by Sex?”: a pooled Normal model versus separate means by sex. More detail on reading the az.compare() table.
  • “Alternative Priors: The Impact of Prior Knowledge” replaced by “Prior Sensitivity: How Much Does the Prior Matter?”. Two priors compared through their exact posteriors, on the 30-subject sample and on the full cohort.
  • The Dirichlet-Categorical Model rewritten. The Dirichlet is presented as the Beta extended to K categories. The ratios of the \alpha_k set the mean. Their sum sets the concentration.
  • PyMC versus Stan comparison expanded.

Chapter 11: Causal Inference

Corrected

  • Why Causality Matters: A Cautionary Tale: the arrhythmia drugs were said to have looked beneficial in observational studies. They were prescribed because they suppressed arrhythmias, which are associated with death after a heart attack. The randomized trial showed that they raised mortality.
  • The Policy Trap: removed the claim that this example, a treatment with no effect, is what happened with the arrhythmia drugs. Those drugs were harmful.
  • Potential Outcomes: The Fundamental Concept: potential outcomes and counterfactuals were described as the same thing. The potential outcomes are C_0 and C_1. The counterfactual is the one that is not observed.
  • A Motivating Case: COVID-19 Vaccination and Hospitalization: in December 2021 the hospitalization rate of the unvaccinated was about 25 per 100,000, not 20.
  • A Motivating Case: COVID-19 Vaccination and Hospitalization: the figure showed a rate of zero for three doses in August 2021, where the source has no data.
  • A Real Example: COVID-19 Vaccination Rates: the tables left out children under 5, who were not eligible for vaccination and count towards the overall rate at 0%. The 5–11 group was named as the one with the lowest vaccination rate.
  • Example “The Paradox in Action”: the comparisons within each age group were read as causal effects without an assumption that makes them so. The example assumes that treatment is as good as randomly assigned within each age group.
  • Self-test problem 1 (Association and Causation): the solution said that the association equals the causal effect under no unmeasured confounding. That holds under random assignment. Under no unmeasured confounding, the causal effect is the adjusted treatment effect.

New

  • Why Causality Matters: A Cautionary Tale: the mortality figures of the arrhythmia trial, with its source.
  • A Real Example: COVID-19 Vaccination Rates: Espoo’s age-group rates, weighted by Finland’s age structure, give 81.0%, above Finland’s 80.2%. The example is linked to the adjustment formula.
  • The Counterfactual Resolution: with no unmeasured confounding given Z, the subgroup comparisons in the data are causal effects.

Chapter 12: Missing Data

Corrected

  • Financial Performance Biases: survivorship bias was attributed to all standard financial indices. It affects databases of fund returns and the indices computed from them.
  • Observed Hedge Fund Performance Biases: the caption of the Fung and Hsieh table said it covers hedge funds and commodity trading advisors. It covers individual hedge funds and funds-of-hedge funds. Its bias columns record whether each return contains a bias.
  • The “Advanced: Multi-Period Sampling Bias” box (Types of Bias in Fund Data): the minimum track record was said to be required by databases. Research studies impose it, and Fung and Hsieh find the resulting bias very small. A recommendation that is not in the paper is removed.
  • A Causal Model for the Missing Data Process: assumption A was said to have been found to hold. The follow-up data support it but cannot prove it.
  • The Multiple Imputation Approach: the MI-MNAR confidence intervals were said to contain the true values for both deaths and hospitalizations. The interval for hospitalizations narrowly misses it.
  • The Multiple Imputation Approach: MI-MNAR was said to perform much better than the other methods. That holds for deaths. For hospitalizations its estimate is as far from the true value as theirs, on the other side.
  • Example “Classifying Sources of Missing Data”: people without phones were classified as possibly censored. Their responses are unobserved in non-random sampling.
  • The “Key Distinction: Sources vs. Mechanisms” box (Missing Data Mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR)): random sensor failure was given as an example of censoring. It is replaced by an untested patient, which can be MAR or MNAR.
  • Ignorability (Computational tab): the spread of 100 simulated slope estimates was called a 95% confidence interval.
  • Ignorability (Computational tab): when missingness depends only on the predictor, complete case analysis was said to bias the intercept and predictions. It does not.
  • Ignorability (Computational tab): complete case regression was said to be biased whenever missingness depends on an auxiliary variable. It is biased only if that variable is related to the outcome beyond what the predictor explains.
  • Simple (Single) Imputation: any imputation from observed values of the same variable was said to reduce variability. Imputing a single value, such as the mean, does.
  • Simple (Single) Imputation: mean-imputed values were said to have zero correlation with other variables. Mean imputation shrinks correlations toward zero.
  • Example “How Mean Imputation Distorts Variance”: the text said 30 values were imputed; the code made 40 missing. The example removes exactly 30 of 100 values, and the variance of the completed data is 70% of that of the observed values.
  • Multiple Imputation (MI): scikit-learn’s IterativeImputer was given as the Python tool. By default it makes a single imputation; MICE in statsmodels is named instead.
  • The “Connection to Bootstrap and Bayesian Methods” box (Multiple Imputation (MI)): Rubin’s rules were called frequentist. They were derived as an approximation to Bayesian posterior inference.
  • Weighting Methods: inverse probability weighting was said to give unbiased estimates. It gives consistent estimates when no one has zero probability of being observed.
  • The “Advanced: Selection Models vs. Pattern-Mixture Models” box (Model-Based Procedures): the FINRISK analysis was given as an example of a selection model. It fits no model of participation given the data.
  • Chapter Connections: mean imputation was said to widen confidence intervals. It narrows them.
  • Python and R Reference: the statsmodels MICE code raised an error.
  • Python and R Reference: method = "pmm" in R was described as applying to continuous variables. It applies to every incomplete variable.

New

  • Simple (Single) Imputation: imputation by prediction with k-nearest neighbours or principal component analysis, stochastic imputation, and the mode for a categorical variable.

Chapter 13: Experimental and Survey Design

Corrected

  • A Cautionary Tale: The 1936 Literary Digest Poll: the poll received about 2.4 million responses, not 2.27 million. That figure counted only the ballots for Landon and Roosevelt.
  • A Cautionary Tale: The 1936 Literary Digest Poll: removed the state-by-state table of the poll’s results. It contained transcription errors, and its columns did not add up to the poll’s totals.
  • What Went Wrong?: the poll was not conducted several months before the election. Ballots were mailed in late summer, and returns were published from early September to the end of October.
  • The Postmortem Evidence: Roosevelt’s 55% among those who received a ballot was said to match his actual 61% reasonably well. The sampling frame did lower his share, but not enough to change the winner.
  • The Verdict: the failure was attributed mainly to nonresponse bias. Squire (1988) attributes about 11 percentage points of the error to the sampling frame and about 7 to nonresponse. Nonresponse is what produced the wrong winner.
  • Sampling Methods: simple random sampling was defined as giving every unit the same chance of selection. Cluster sampling can do that too. In simple random sampling, every possible sample of n units is equally likely.
  • Type S and Type M Errors: below 10% power, significant results were said to be likely to have the wrong sign. The Type S error rate is about 5% at 10% power and approaches 50% only as power falls towards 5%.
  • Type S and Type M Errors: at 80% power the Type S error rate is about 10^{-6}, not 0.001%.
  • Self-test problem 2 (Experimental Design), part (c): the Latin square requires no interactions among any of the three factors. An interaction between school and difficulty also biases the estimated effect of method.

New

  • Surveys and Inference: how random sampling from a finite population fits the IID model of the earlier chapters, and why a flawed sampling frame or nonresponse gives a bias that does not shrink as the sample grows.

Revised

  • Type S and Type M Errors: the figure of the Type S error rate and the exaggeration ratio covers the whole range of power, from 0.05 to 1.

### [Chapter 13: Experimental and Survey Design](chapters/13-experimental-survey-design.qmd)

**Corrected**

- *A Cautionary Tale: The 1936 Literary Digest Poll*: the poll received about
  2.4 million responses, not 2.27 million. That figure counted only the
  ballots for Landon and Roosevelt.
- *A Cautionary Tale: The 1936 Literary Digest Poll*: removed the
  state-by-state table of the poll's results. It contained transcription
  errors, and its columns did not add up to the poll's totals.
- *What Went Wrong?*: the poll was not conducted several months before the
  election. Ballots were mailed in late summer, and returns were published
  from early September to the end of October.
- *The Postmortem Evidence*: Roosevelt's 55% among those who received a ballot
  was said to match his actual 61% reasonably well. The sampling frame did
  lower his share, but not enough to change the winner.
- *The Verdict*: the failure was attributed mainly to nonresponse bias.
  Squire (1988) attributes about 11 percentage points of the error to the
  sampling frame and about 7 to nonresponse. Nonresponse is what produced the
  wrong winner.
- *Sampling Methods*: simple random sampling was defined as giving every unit
  the same chance of selection. Cluster sampling can do that too. In simple
  random sampling, every possible sample of $n$ units is equally likely.
- *Type S and Type M Errors*: below 10% power, significant results were said
  to be likely to have the wrong sign. The Type S error rate is about 5% at
  10% power and approaches 50% only as power falls towards 5%.
- *Type S and Type M Errors*: at 80% power the Type S error rate is about
  $10^{-6}$, not 0.001%.
- Self-test problem 2 (Experimental Design), part (c): the Latin square
  requires no interactions among any of the three factors. An interaction
  between school and difficulty also biases the estimated effect of method.

**New**

- *Surveys and Inference*: how random sampling from a finite population fits
  the IID model of the earlier chapters, and why a flawed sampling frame or
  nonresponse gives a bias that does not shrink as the sample grows.

**Revised**

- *Type S and Type M Errors*: the figure of the Type S error rate and the
  exaggeration ratio covers the whole range of power, from 0.05 to 1.
