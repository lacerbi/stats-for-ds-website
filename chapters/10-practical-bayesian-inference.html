10.2 Introduction: Why Probabilistic Programming?

10.2.1 Motivation: Bayesian Inference in the Real World

In 2016, the LIGO team announced a stunning discovery: they had detected gravitational waves from colliding black holes, confirming a century-old prediction by Einstein. The detection itself was remarkable – finding a tiny wobble in space-time buried in overwhelming noise. But once they knew something was there, an equally challenging question arose: what exactly had they observed? What were the masses of the black holes? How far away were they? Where in the sky did the merger occur?

This is where Bayesian inference became essential. The team couldn’t just give single “best guess” answers – they needed to quantify their uncertainty about each property by computing the posterior distribution. This mirrors what we saw in Chapter 8 with the search for Air France Flight 447, where Bayesian methods succeeded by combining multiple uncertain sources of information rather than relying on any single piece of evidence. Similarly, for LIGO, using Bayesian methods allowed them to say things like “we’re 90% confident the total mass was between 60 and 70 solar masses” rather than just “the mass was 65 solar masses.”

The LIGO team used specialized inference software developed over many years specifically for gravitational wave astronomy. But here’s the key insight: the same fundamental challenges they faced – combining uncertain information, quantifying confidence in conclusions, handling complex models that do not admit closed-form solutions – appear everywhere in modern data science.

While it used to be the case, it’s not very efficient for every researcher to spend years building custom inference software for their specific problems. That’s where general-purpose probabilistic programming tools come in. They let us tackle similarly complex problems whether we’re:

  • Aggregating opinion polls to predict election outcomes (like The Economist’s 2020 US presidential forecast, written in Stan by The Economist together with the statisticians Andrew Gelman and Merlin Heidemanns)
  • Analyzing genomic data to identify bacterial strains in microbiome studies
  • Detecting anomalies in sensor networks or financial transactions
  • Personalizing recommendations while quantifying uncertainty

In all these problems, we need methods that can handle complex models with dozens or even thousands of parameters, incorporate prior knowledge, and – crucially – tell us how uncertain the results are. The promise of probabilistic programming tools is to allow the researcher to focus on defining the model, and the machinery should automatically take care of the inference.1

10.2.2 From Models to Inference

The gap between statistical theory and practice used to be enormous. Suppose you write down a Bayesian model on paper:

\begin{aligned} \text{Prior: } & \theta \sim \pi(\theta) \\ \text{Likelihood: } & X \mid \theta \sim f(X \mid \theta) \\ \text{Posterior: } & \theta \mid X \sim \frac{f(X \mid \theta) \pi(\theta)}{\int f(X \mid \theta') \pi(\theta') d\theta'} \end{aligned}

How do you solve this? Outside of a few special cases with conjugate priors (like Beta-Binomial or Normal-Normal from Chapter 8), we can’t write down a formula for the posterior distribution.

So how do we actually do Bayesian inference in practice? One common solution is that we don’t compute the posterior distribution directly – we draw samples from it. Using algorithms like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), we generate hundreds or thousands of samples \theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_m that, collectively, represent the posterior distribution.

Samples are Practical Representations of Distributions

Samples are a very convenient way of representing a probability distribution, whether discrete or continuous. Computing any statistic via samples is straightforward:

  • the mean is np.mean(samples)
  • a 95% credible interval is np.percentile(samples, [2.5, 97.5])
  • p(\theta > 0) is just np.mean(samples > 0)
  • the distribution of some complex function g(\theta) is g(samples)

The tradeoff is clear: we lose analytical precision but gain computational flexibility.

This is the key insight of practical Bayesian inference: we approximate the posterior through samples rather than computing it exactly. But implementing MCMC correctly used to require deep expertise – you needed to code your own sampler, tune it properly, and verify convergence. If you got any of these steps wrong, your results would be unreliable.

Probabilistic programming makes this much more accessible. With tools like PyMC or Stan, you write your model in code that mirrors the mathematical notation:

  • PyMC
  • Stan
with pm.Model() as model:
    # Prior
    theta = pm.Normal("theta", mu=0, sigma=1)
    # Likelihood
    x = pm.Normal("x", mu=theta, sigma=1, observed=data)
    # Inference happens automatically!
    trace = pm.sample()
# Model specification (in .stan file)
model {
  theta ~ normal(0, 1);        // Prior
  x ~ normal(theta, 1);         // Likelihood  
}

# In R:
fit <- stan(file="model.stan", data=list(x=data))

The system handles all the computational heavy lifting: computing gradients, tuning samplers, running diagnostics, and producing posterior samples. Ideally, you can focus on the science, not the numerical methods.

10.2.3 Practical Bayesian Inference via PyMC

This chapter teaches practical Bayesian inference through PyMC, the leading probabilistic programming framework in Python. We chose PyMC because it:

  • Integrates seamlessly with the Python scientific stack (NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib)
  • Provides state-of-the-art MCMC samplers like NUTS (No-U-Turn Sampler) that work well on complex models
  • Includes comprehensive diagnostics to ensure your results are reliable
  • Scales from simple examples to production models with thousands of parameters
  • Runs on fast modern backends: the same model can be sampled by samplers built on JAX or Numba by changing one argument, and JAX also runs on GPUs

Stan is the other widely used tool, with a vibrant developer and user community and a long track record. A Stan model is written in Stan’s own modeling language and compiled to C++, which makes it fast but adds a second language and a compilation step to the workflow. A PyMC model is ordinary Python code, which makes PyMC easier to pick up for data scientists already working in Python. Speed used to be the main argument for Stan, and the JAX- and Numba-based samplers have narrowed that gap.2

10.3 MCMC Foundations and Diagnostics

10.3.1 MCMC Basics

As we mentioned earlier, practical Bayesian inference hinges on a simple but powerful idea: instead of computing integrals analytically (usually impossible), we draw samples from the posterior distribution and use those samples to approximate any quantity we need.

We primarily obtain these samples using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). MCMC is not a single algorithm, but a broad family of different methods that allow us to sample from a target probability distribution \pi(\theta), provided its unnormalized density.

The Target: Unnormalized Posteriors

Our goal is to sample from the posterior distribution:

f(\theta \mid x^n) = \frac{f(x^n \mid \theta) f(\theta)}{\int f(x^n \mid \theta) f(\theta) d\theta}

The denominator – the marginal likelihood – is typically intractable. But here’s the fundamental point: MCMC methods only need the unnormalized posterior:

f(\theta \mid x^n) \propto f(x^n \mid \theta) f(\theta)

This is exactly what we can compute easily! The likelihood times the prior gives us an unnormalized density that’s proportional to the posterior, and we can feed this to MCMC algorithms.3

Markov Chains: From Local Moves to Global Exploration

MCMC algorithms iteratively construct a stochastic sequence – a Markov chain \theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_m – that explores the parameter space. In the chain, each new state depends only on the current state (the Markov property):

\theta_{m+1} \sim p(\theta_{m+1} | \theta_m)

where p(\theta_{m+1} | \theta_m) depends on the transition kernel unique to each MCMC algorithm. Many algorithms use local transitions (close to the current point), but locality is not required and modern methods can efficiently explore far from the current point. This probability distribution is designed following mathematical rules such that the stationary distribution of the produced chain is our target posterior.4

Think of it like a guided random walk through parameter space: the algorithm is built in such a way that it tends to spend more time in high-probability regions (where the posterior is large) and less time in low-probability regions, producing samples that represent the posterior distribution.

In modern implementations, MCMC sampling is typically split into two phases:

  1. Warm-up/Adaptation (also called burn-in): Starting from an arbitrary point, the algorithm moves toward higher-probability regions, often adjusting its own settings as it goes. We discard these early draws.5
  2. Sampling: With settings fixed, the chain explores the high-probability region and we keep these draws as our posterior samples.
MCMC Samples Are Correlated

Consecutive MCMC draws are not independent because each step builds on the previous one. This is normal and does not invalidate the correctness of the algorithm, but it means you may need more iterations to get a good picture of the posterior. Use standard diagnostics (built into most libraries) to detect if you have collected enough samples and, if needed, run longer or use more efficient samplers. Even advanced methods still show some correlation for challenging problems.

10.3.2 Convergence and \hat{R}

MCMC algorithms are constructed such that they are mathematically guaranteed to converge in distribution to the target (Chapter 3): as m \to \infty, the distribution of \theta_m approaches \pi(\theta).6 However, this guarantee comes with two critical caveats:

  1. We can only run for finite time: While convergence is guaranteed asymptotically, in practice we must stop after a finite number of iterations.
  2. No finite-time proof: For most MCMC algorithms, it’s impossible to prove that convergence has been achieved after any specific number of iterations.

This creates a fundamental challenge: How do we know if our MCMC sampler has converged to the correct distribution? While we can never be completely certain, we can detect when it almost certainly has not converged by running multiple chains and checking whether they’re all exploring the same distribution. The most common diagnostic for this is the \hat{R} statistic.

  • Intuitive
  • Mathematical
  • Computational

MCMC tries to generate samples from an unknown probability distribution (the posterior). Since we can’t sample directly, we use a clever trick: we create a random walk that naturally spends more time in high-probability regions and less time in low-probability regions.

But how do we know our random walk has found the right distribution? We run multiple independent walks (chains) starting from different places and check if they all end up with the same behavior:

  • All chains visit the same regions with the same frequency → No sign of problems ✓
  • Different chains are stuck in different regions or show different patterns → Not converged ✗
  • Some chains are still moving away from their starting points → Still warming up ✗

The key insight: agreement between independent chains is only moderate evidence that sampling went well, since all the chains can miss the same region of the posterior. Disagreement is a clear sign we need to run longer or fix our sampler.

The most common convergence diagnostic is the \(\hat{R}\) statistic (Potential Scale Reduction Factor), which quantifies whether independent chains have converged to the same distribution.

Conceptually, \(\hat{R}\) can be understood as:

\[\hat{R} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\text{Between-chain variance} + \text{Within-chain variance}}{\text{Within-chain variance}}}\]

This is a simplification – modern software computes rank-normalized split-\(\hat{R}\) [@vehtari2021rank], which splits chains, applies rank normalization for robustness, and uses more sophisticated variance estimators. Use library implementations rather than coding this yourself.

For well-converged chains:

  • Between-chain variance ≈ Within-chain variance
  • Therefore \(\hat{R} \approx 1.0\)
  • Practical threshold: \(\hat{R} \leq 1.01\) indicates convergence

Values above 1.01 suggest the chains haven’t fully mixed and are exploring different regions of the parameter space.

In practice, checking convergence involves both numerical diagnostics and visual inspection. Here’s what good versus poor convergence may look like:

The left panel shows an example of converged chains: all four chains quickly found the target distribution and are exploring the same region, creating overlapping “fuzzy caterpillar” patterns. Each chain visits similar values with similar frequency - this is what we want to see.

The right panel shows an example of non-converged chains: they started far apart and haven’t yet found a common distribution. Some chains are stuck in different regions (perhaps different modes), while others are still wandering. These samples are not representative of the true posterior - using them for inference would be misleading!

The key insight: Good convergence shows all chains “agreeing” about the posterior distribution, exploring the same regions with the same frequency.

10.4 Hello, PyMC: Building and Sampling a Simple Model

10.4.1 Defining Probabilistic Models: Joint → Conditional

Let’s understand how probabilistic programming languages represent models. Any joint distribution can be factorized using the chain rule:

f(y_1, \dots, y_n) = f(y_1 \mid y_2, \dots, y_n) \cdot f(y_2 \mid y_3, \dots, y_n) \cdots f(y_n)

This factorization works for any ordering of variables. In practice, we choose an ordering that makes the conditional distributions simple to specify.

For a basic Bayesian model with parameters \theta and data X, the simplest factorization is:

f(\theta, X) = f(X \mid \theta) \cdot f(\theta)

This is the familiar likelihood times prior, which we can express using sampling notation:

\begin{aligned} \theta &\sim f(\theta) && \text{(prior)} \\ X \mid \theta &\sim f(X \mid \theta) && \text{(likelihood)} \end{aligned}

In probabilistic programming, as in Bayesian statistics, there is no fundamental distinction between parameters and data: both are random variables that we represent using this sampling notation. The only difference is that data values are observed (fixed to measured values), while parameter values are unobserved (to be inferred).

Example: Measuring Heights with Unknown Mean and Variance

Consider measuring heights in a population where we assume both mean and variance are independent and unknown. Based on our prior over the parameters, our generative model is:

\begin{aligned} \mu &\sim \text{Normal}(170, 20) && \text{(mean height in cm)} \\ \sigma &\sim \text{HalfNormal}(10) && \text{(standard deviation in cm)} \\ \text{heights} \mid \mu, \sigma &\sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma) && \text{(individual heights in cm)} \end{aligned}

The joint distribution is: f(\mu, \sigma, \text{heights}) = f(\text{heights} \mid \mu, \sigma) \cdot f(\sigma) \cdot f(\mu)

This translates to code as:

  • PyMC
  • Stan
mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=170, sigma=20)
sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=10)
heights = pm.Normal("heights", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=data)
parameters {
  real mu;
  real<lower=0> sigma;    // Constrained to be positive
}
model {
  mu ~ normal(170, 20);         
  sigma ~ normal(0, 10);    // Truncated at 0 due to constraint
  heights ~ normal(mu, sigma);  
}

The power of probabilistic programming is that this same approach scales to much more complex models – hierarchical structures, time series, mixture models – all using the same basic principle: we describe the generative process, and the system handles the inference.

Normal Distribution Notation For This Chapter

To align with programming libraries and avoid confusion, this chapter uses a different notation than previous chapters for the normal pdf:

  • This chapter (programming notation): X \sim \text{Normal}(170, 20) where 20 is the standard deviation.
  • Previous chapters (mathematical notation): X \sim \mathcal{N}(170, 20^2) where 20^2 = 400 is the variance.

Throughout this chapter, we use \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma) to denote a normal distribution with mean \mu and standard deviation \sigma. This matches the parameterization in PyMC, Stan, and most programming languages.

10.4.2 Estimating the Parameters of a Normal Distribution

For a starter, let’s implement the classic conjugate model from Chapter 8: estimating the mean of a normal distribution with known variance. We’ll use real cholesterol data from the Framingham Heart Study. For this example, we’ll work with a random subsample of 30 subjects, which allows us to better visualize the influence of the prior and the uncertainty in our estimates.

Why Use MCMC for a Conjugate Model?

This normal-normal model has a known analytical solution (see Chapter 8). We use it here to demonstrate MCMC because testing computational methods on problems with known solutions is fundamental practice in data science – it lets us verify our tools work correctly before tackling problems where no closed-form solution exists.

Step 2: Model Specification

Now we’ll specify our Bayesian model, first in statistical language, then in probabilistic programming language.

Model Specification
  • Data: Cholesterol measurements X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma) for i = 1, \ldots, 30
  • Prior: \mu \sim \text{Normal}(210, 40) mg/dL
  • Known parameter: \sigma = 45 mg/dL (from external population studies)

This example treats \sigma as known from external calibration studies of cholesterol variability in similar populations. This simplification allows us to focus on the mechanics of MCMC sampling. In practice, if \sigma is unknown, it would be given its own prior (e.g., HalfNormal) and estimated jointly with \mu.

  • PyMC
  • Stan
import pymc as pm

# For this example, we use a known standard deviation from external calibration
# This value comes from population studies of cholesterol variability
sigma_known = 45.0  # mg/dL - from external population calibration
print(f"Using known population standard deviation: {sigma_known:.1f} mg/dL")

# Build the model
with pm.Model() as normal_model:
    # Prior: Normal centered at 210 mg/dL with SD of 40
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=210, sigma=40)

    # Likelihood: observed data with known standard deviation
    y_obs = pm.Normal("y_obs", mu=mu, sigma=sigma_known, observed=y)

We’ve defined two key components: a prior distribution for our unknown parameter mu, and a likelihood that connects our observed data y to that parameter through observed=y. The model is now fully specified and ready for inference.

Stan uses a different approach than PyMC: it requires explicit declaration of data types and uses a separate modeling language. The model is divided into three blocks:

  • data: Declares the observed data and known parameters (here: n, y, sigma)
  • parameters: Declares the unknown parameters to estimate (here: just mu)
  • model: Specifies the prior and likelihood distributions

Here we compile the model to C++ using stan_model(). This compilation step takes time but produces an optimized executable. We’ll run the actual sampling in Step 4.

Choosing Priors: Art Meets Science

Our prior \text{Normal}(210, 40) reflects medical knowledge that total cholesterol typically ranges from 130-290 mg/dL in adults. The mean of 210 represents a slightly elevated but common level, while the standard deviation of 40 allows the prior to cover the plausible range without being overly restrictive.

Prior selection balances several considerations:

  • Domain expertise: What values are biologically/physically plausible?
  • Available information: Previous studies, expert opinion, or pilot data
  • Computational stability: Some priors work better with certain samplers
  • Sensitivity analysis: How much do results change with different reasonable priors?

While the Normal models in this chapter use informative priors, remember that “weakly informative” priors on standardized data (like \text{Normal}(0, 10)) are often a safe default when domain knowledge is limited.

Understanding Stan’s Structure (Optional)

Stan is a statically typed probabilistic programming language that takes a different approach from PyMC. Here are the key concepts:

Type Declarations: Stan requires explicit type declarations for all variables:

  • int for integers, real for real numbers
  • vector[n] for real vectors of length n
  • array[n] real y for arrays of n real numbers
  • Constraints like <lower=0> enforce positivity

The Three Essential Blocks:

  1. data: Fixed inputs including observations and known parameters
  2. parameters: Unknown variables to be estimated
  3. model: Prior and likelihood specifications

Compilation Model: Stan compiles your model to optimized C++ code, which means:

  • Initial compilation may take substantial time (several minutes depending on model complexity)
  • Once compiled, the model can be reused with different data without recompiling
  • Hyperparameters (like prior means) should be passed as data if you want to experiment with them without recompiling

Vectorization: Stan automatically vectorizes operations. These are equivalent:

// Vectorized (preferred)
y ~ normal(mu, sigma);

// Loop version (avoid)
for (i in 1:n) {
  y[i] ~ normal(mu, sigma);
}

What’s Actually Happening During Sampling?

Modern probabilistic programming uses an algorithm called NUTS (No-U-Turn Sampler) that automatically explores the posterior distribution. Two key features:

Multiple chains: PyMC runs multiple independent chains (typically 4), each starting from a different random initial value. This parallelizes computation (using multiple CPU cores) and is essential for convergence diagnostics - we need multiple chains to verify they all find the same posterior distribution. In our example, we’re using the default number of chains (likely 4 on most systems).

Two-phase sampling: Each chain goes through:

  1. Tuning/Warmup: The sampler learns the “shape” of your posterior – where to look, how big steps to take. These samples are discarded. PyMC’s default is typically 1000 iterations, but we use tune=500 here for speed.
  2. Sampling: Using what it learned, the sampler collects the actual samples we’ll use for inference. Only these post-warmup samples are returned and used for all subsequent analysis. PyMC’s default is typically 1000 draws, but we use draws=500 here for speed.

With our settings (500 post-warmup draws per chain, likely 4 chains), we get 2000 total posterior samples. The iteration counts aren’t rules – complex models might need thousands, simple ones might need fewer. The key innovation is that NUTS adapts automatically, unlike older methods that required manual tuning for each problem.

Step 5: Convergence Diagnostics

Before trusting our posterior samples, we must check for signs that the MCMC sampler has not converged. Recall from the discussion of convergence and \hat{R} that convergence means our chains have found and are exploring the target distribution.

All diagnostics below are computed using only the post-warmup samples – the warmup/tuning iterations have already been discarded. With our settings (4 chains, 500 samples each), we’re analyzing 2000 posterior draws total.

Numerical Diagnostics

First, let’s check the key convergence statistics. Modern MCMC diagnostics focus on two critical measures:

\hat{R} (R-hat): Compares variation between chains to variation within chains. Values close to 1.0 mean that the chains agree with each other. The threshold \hat{R} \leq 1.01 has become standard based on extensive empirical studies (Vehtari et al. 2021) showing it reliably detects non-convergence.

ESS (Effective Sample Size): Because MCMC samples are autocorrelated, 1000 samples don’t provide as much information as 1000 independent draws. ESS estimates the equivalent number of independent samples. The conventional threshold of ESS > 400 total (or 100 per chain with 4 chains) typically ensures stable estimates for posterior means and 95% credible intervals, though more extreme quantiles may require higher ESS.7

10.5.2 The Dirichlet-Categorical Model

When we have categorical data, we need a different statistical model. The natural choice is:

  • Data: Each observation belongs to one of K categories
  • Parameter: A probability vector \mathbf{p} = (p_1, \ldots, p_K) where p_k is the probability of category k
  • Constraint: The probabilities must sum to 1: \sum_{k=1}^K p_k = 1
  • Likelihood: Each observation is a draw from the Categorical distribution, X_i \mid \mathbf{p} \sim \text{Categorical}(\mathbf{p}), meaning \mathbb{P}(X_i = k) = p_k

This constraint means \mathbf{p} lives on the probability simplex – the set of all valid probability distributions over K categories. For our 3-category cholesterol data, this is a triangular surface in 3D space where any point represents a valid probability distribution. Since the three coordinates always sum to 1, we can draw the triangle flat, as in the figure below. Each corner is the distribution that puts all its probability on one category, the centre is (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), and a point gives a category more probability the further it lies from the edge opposite that category’s corner.

The Dirichlet distribution is the natural prior for probability vectors:

\mathbf{p} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_K), \quad \alpha_k > 0

It extends the Beta distribution of Chapter 8 from two categories to K. With K = 2 the probability vector is (p, 1 - p), and \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2) is the same as p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2). The interpretation carries over too: just as \alpha and \beta in the Beta act as pseudo-counts of prior successes and failures, \alpha_k acts as a pseudo-count of prior observations in category k.

The parameter vector \boldsymbol{\alpha} does two separate jobs, one through its ratios and one through its sum. Write \alpha_0 = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k for the sum:

  • The ratios set the mean: \mathbb{E}[p_k] = \alpha_k / \alpha_0. For example, \text{Dirichlet}(2, 4, 8) and \text{Dirichlet}(1, 2, 4) have the same mean, (1/7, 2/7, 4/7).
  • The sum sets the concentration: \alpha_0 is the total number of pseudo-observations, and the larger it is, the more tightly \mathbf{p} clusters around its mean. Writing m_k = \alpha_k/\alpha_0 for the mean of p_k, its variance is \mathbb{V}(p_k) = m_k(1 - m_k)/(\alpha_0 + 1).

Two special cases are worth knowing. With all \alpha_k = 1 the Dirichlet is uniform over the simplex: every probability vector is equally likely. With all \alpha_k < 1 it favours sparse vectors, pushing its mass towards the edges and corners of the simplex, where one or two categories take nearly all the probability.

def simplex_xy(p):
    """Map probability vectors (rows of p) to 2D points in the triangle."""
    p = np.atleast_2d(p)
    return p[:, 1] + 0.5 * p[:, 2], (np.sqrt(3) / 2) * p[:, 2]

Dirichlet-Categorical Conjugacy

The Dirichlet and Categorical form a conjugate pair, just like the Beta and Bernoulli:

  • Prior: \mathbf{p} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
  • Likelihood: Observe n_k instances of category k, out of n = \sum_k n_k observations
  • Posterior: \mathbf{p} \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{n}), where \mathbf{n} = (n_1, \ldots, n_K)

The posterior simply adds the observed counts to the prior pseudo-counts! Its mean, (\alpha_k + n_k)/(\alpha_0 + n), is a compromise between the prior mean \alpha_k/\alpha_0 and the observed proportion n_k/n, exactly as for the Beta.

10.6 Bayesian Model Comparison

So far we have fitted one model at a time, but often there are several candidate models for the same data and we need to choose between them. Here is a concrete question from the Framingham data: do women and men differ in their mean cholesterol? Each possible answer is a model:

  • Model 1 (pooled): everyone shares one mean, X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma).
  • Model 2 (by sex): women and men each have their own mean, X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_{s_i}, \sigma), where s_i \in \{\text{female}, \text{male}\} is the sex of subject i.

Model 1 is the special case of Model 2 with \mu_{\text{female}} = \mu_{\text{male}}, so choosing between them is the Bayesian counterpart of the two-sample test in Chapter 7 (Example: Comparing Two Means). Model 2 is also a regression of cholesterol on a single binary predictor, as in Chapter 9.

In a Bayesian context, testing is usually performed via model comparison. The aim is to infer which of multiple models \mathcal{M}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{M}_k is the best fit for the data. What “best” means depends on what we are willing to assume about reality.

10.6.1 Two Philosophies of Model Comparison

Bayesian model comparison comes in two fundamentally different flavors, depending on what we believe about reality (Kelter 2021):

\mathcal{M}-closed perspective: Assumes one of our candidate models is actually the true data-generating process. The set \mathcal{M} of models is “closed” because it contains the truth – we just need to figure out which one it is. This classical Bayesian approach treats model selection like a multiple choice question where one answer is definitely correct. We compute posterior probabilities over models, \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i \mid X^n = x^n), asking: “Given the data, what’s the probability that Model i is the true model?”

\mathcal{M}-open perspective: Acknowledges that all our models are wrong – approximations of a reality that’s more complex than anything we can write down. The model set is “open” because the true process lies outside it. Since no model is true, we can’t ask “which one is correct?” Instead, we ask “which approximation makes the best predictions?” This is the more realistic perspective for most real-world problems.

In practice, the \mathcal{M}-open perspective is more realistic. Statistician George E. P. Box famously said “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” Our two models are clearly simplifications: cholesterol is not exactly normally distributed (its distribution is skewed to the right), and many factors besides sex affect it. So we’ll focus on predictive performance.

10.6.2 Model Comparison in the \mathcal{M}-Closed Setting

In the \mathcal{M}-closed approach, we apply Bayes’ theorem directly to models:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i \mid X^n = x^n) \propto f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_i) \cdot \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i)

The key quantity here is f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_i), called the marginal likelihood or evidence, which integrates the likelihood over all possible parameter values weighted by the prior:

f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_i) = \int f(x^n \mid \theta, \mathcal{M}_i) f(\theta \mid \mathcal{M}_i) \, d\theta

This integral averages how well the model explains the data across all parameter values the prior considers plausible.

To compare two models, we often compute the ratio of their posterior probabilities:

\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_1 \mid x^n)}{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_2 \mid x^n)} = \frac{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_2)} \cdot \frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_1)}{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_2)}

When we assign equal prior probabilities to both models (no preference a priori), this simplifies to just the ratio of marginal likelihoods, called the Bayes factor:

\text{BF}_{12} = \frac{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_2)}

A Bayes factor of 10 means the data are 10 times more likely under Model 1 than Model 2.

A Note on Prior Sensitivity

Bayes factors are widely used and mathematically principled, but they do have an important limitation: they are sensitive to the prior, especially its width.

Making a prior twice as wide can dramatically reduce the marginal likelihood, even if the posterior barely changes. This happens because the likelihood is usually much more concentrated than the prior. A broader prior “wastes” probability mass on unlikely parameter values, reducing the evidence. In our example, widening the priors on the means would penalise Model 2, which has two of them, more than Model 1, and so shift the Bayes factor towards Model 1 without changing either posterior much.

This sensitivity doesn’t make Bayes factors wrong – they correctly answer the question “which model is more probable?” under the \mathcal{M}-closed assumption. But it does mean you need confidence in your prior specification, which can be challenging in practice.

10.6.3 The Alternative: Predictive Model Comparison

Given this sensitivity and the fact that all our models are approximations anyway, this chapter focuses on the \mathcal{M}-open approach. Instead of asking “which model is true?” (when none are), we ask “which model makes better predictions?” This shifts our focus from model probability to predictive performance. It is the same principle as model selection in Chapter 9: judge a model by how well it predicts new data, not by how well it fits the data it was trained on.

Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD)

To predict a new observation x^\star after seeing data X^n, we use the posterior predictive distribution:

p(x^\star \mid X^n) = \int p(x^\star \mid \theta) p(\theta \mid X^n) \, d\theta

Taking the logarithm gives the log predictive density (LPD) for that specific observation:

\text{LPD}(x^\star) = \log p(x^\star \mid X^n)

But LPD for a single point isn’t enough – it depends on which x^\star we happen to see. We need the expected log predictive density (ELPD) over all possible future observations:

\text{ELPD} = \mathbb{E}_{x^\star \sim p_{\text{true}}}[\text{LPD}(x^\star)]

This measures average predictive performance, which is what we actually care about for model comparison.

In practice with MCMC samples \theta_i \sim p(\theta \mid X^n), we approximate LPD as:

\widehat{\text{LPD}}(x^\star) = \log \left( \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m p(x^\star \mid \theta_i) \right)

Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation for ELPD

We can’t compute true ELPD without knowing p_{\text{true}} and having infinite future data. Scoring the model on the data it was fitted to would be too optimistic, just as training error is in Chapter 9. The solution is leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV), which Chapter 9 introduced with squared prediction error as the score: treat each existing observation as a “future” test point for a model trained without it. Here, each held-out point is scored by its log predictive density instead.

For each observation x_k, we’d ideally:

  1. Obtain the posterior p(\theta \mid X_{-k}) using all data except x_k
  2. Compute the posterior predictive p(x_k \mid X_{-k}) = \int p(x_k \mid \theta) p(\theta \mid X_{-k}) d\theta
  3. Sum over all k to estimate ELPD

This gives ELPD-LOO:

\text{ELPD-LOO} = \sum_{k=1}^n \log p(x_k \mid X_{-k})

Actually refitting the model n times would be computationally prohibitive. Fortunately, we can approximate this efficiently by reweighting our existing posterior samples:

\text{ELPD-LOO} = \sum_{k=1}^n \log \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^m w_i^{(k)} p(x_k \mid \theta_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^m w_i^{(k)}} \right)

where the weights w_i^{(k)} are computed using Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS). This is what ArviZ’s loo() function implements. It needs the pointwise log-likelihoods \log p(x_k \mid \theta_i) for every observation x_k and posterior draw \theta_i, which PyMC stores only when asked, by calling pm.sample() with idata_kwargs={"log_likelihood": True}.

Important: Don’t Compare Individual Standard Errors!

The se column shows each model’s individual ELPD uncertainty. You cannot determine if models are significantly different by checking if the intervals \text{elpd\_loo} \pm 2\,\text{se} overlap - that would be statistically incorrect.

Why? Both models are fitted to the same data, so their ELPD estimates are correlated (they make similar errors on the same hard-to-predict observations). The proper comparison uses the standard error of the difference, dse, which az.compare() computes for us.

To decide whether a difference is meaningful, we use the Wald test of Chapter 7. Treating elpd_diff as approximately Normal with standard error dse, the difference is significant at the 5% level if the interval \text{elpd\_diff} \pm 2\,\text{dse} does not include 0, that is, if \text{elpd\_diff} > 2\,\text{dse}.8

With 30 subjects, the pooled model ranks first, and the by-sex model’s elpd_diff is below 2\,\text{dse}: the data cannot tell the two models apart. The women in our sample average about 13 mg/dL more than the men, but with a standard deviation of around 60 mg/dL in this sample, a gap of that size easily arises by chance in groups of 18 and 12 people. The p_loo column shows the price of Model 2’s extra mean. An extra parameter raises ELPD-LOO only if it improves the predictions for held-out points by more than the extra estimation noise costs, and here it does not. AIC makes the same trade-off explicit in Chapter 9: on the log-likelihood scale of ELPD-LOO, its penalty is one unit per parameter, and p_loo plays the role of the parameter count.

Advanced Topic: Hierarchical Models

While beyond this chapter’s scope, hierarchical models deserve mention as they’re ubiquitous in modern Bayesian practice. These models have parameters that themselves follow probability distributions with hyperparameters:

\begin{aligned} y_i &\sim f(y_i \mid \theta_i) \\ \theta_i &\sim g(\theta_i \mid \phi) \\ \phi &\sim h(\phi) \end{aligned}

This creates “partial pooling” – groups share information through the common hyperparameters \phi, but maintain their own parameters \theta_i. Examples include:

  • Clinical trials with patient-specific effects
  • Educational assessments across schools
  • Time series with varying parameters

For comprehensive treatment, see Gelman et al.’s Bayesian Data Analysis or the Stan manual’s case studies.

10.7.2 The Big Picture

This chapter revealed three fundamental insights:

1. Samples Are Enough: We don’t need closed-form posteriors. MCMC samples let us compute any quantity of interest – means, credible intervals, posterior predictive distributions. This liberates us from conjugacy constraints.

2. Automation Enables Exploration: Probabilistic programming handles the computational heavy lifting (gradients, tuning, diagnostics), letting us focus on modeling choices rather than numerical methods. The barrier to trying different models has never been lower.

3. The Standard Workflow Works: Prior predictive → Fit → Diagnose → Posterior analysis → Model comparison. This systematic approach scales from toy problems to production models with hundreds of parameters.   1. Ignoring convergence warnings: Never proceed with \hat{R} > 1.01 or low ESS – fix sampling issues first.
  2. Misinterpreting ELPD-LOO: Higher ELPD means better predictions, not that the model is “true.”
  3. Discarding uncertainty: Report credible intervals, not just posterior means.
  4. Complexity before comprehension: Master simple models before attempting hierarchical structures.

10.7.5 Self-Test Problems

  1. Interpreting Convergence Diagnostics: You run a model and see:

           mean    sd  hdi_3%  hdi_97%  r_hat  ess_bulk
theta  5.23  8.91   -12.3    22.7    1.43      47

    What’s wrong and how would you fix it?

    Solution

    \hat{R} = 1.43 >> 1.01 means chains haven’t converged. ESS = 47 << 400 means too few effective samples. Try: longer chains (tune=2000), higher target_accept (0.95), or better priors to avoid flat regions.

  2. Prior vs Data Influence: Your prior is \mu \sim \text{Normal}(210, 40) for cholesterol. You observe \bar{x} = 230 from n=30 samples with known \sigma=35. Will the posterior mean be closer to 210 or 230? Why?

    Solution

    Compare precisions: prior precision \tau_0^{-2} = 1/40^2 \approx 0.000625, data precision n/\sigma^2 = 30/35^2 \approx 0.0245. Since data precision >> prior precision, the posterior will be pulled much closer to \bar{x} = 230.

  3. Dirichlet-Categorical Conjugacy: You have a Dirichlet(1, 1, 1) prior for three categories. You observe counts (10, 15, 5). What’s the posterior distribution?

    Solution

    Dirichlet-Categorical conjugacy means you just add the counts to the prior parameters: posterior is Dirichlet(1+10, 1+15, 1+5) = Dirichlet(11, 16, 6). The posterior mean for category k is \alpha_k / \sum \alpha_i = 11/33, 16/33, 6/33.

  4. Interpreting ELPD-LOO: az.compare() ranks Model A first, and Model B’s row shows elpd_diff = 3.5 with dse = 1.8. Is the difference meaningful?

    Solution

    The difference is 3.5 in favor of Model A, with dse (standard error of the difference) = 1.8. Since 3.5 < 2×1.8 = 3.6, the difference is not statistically significant. The models perform similarly; neither is clearly better. Note: Use dse (difference SE), not se (individual model SE)!

## Introduction: Why Probabilistic Programming?

### Motivation: Bayesian Inference in the Real World

In 2016, the LIGO team announced a stunning discovery: they had detected gravitational waves from colliding black holes, confirming a century-old prediction by Einstein. The detection itself was remarkable -- finding a tiny wobble in space-time buried in overwhelming noise. But once they knew something was there, an equally challenging question arose: what exactly had they observed? What were the masses of the black holes? How far away were they? Where in the sky did the merger occur? 

This is where Bayesian inference became essential. The team couldn't just give single "best guess" answers -- they needed to quantify their uncertainty about each property by computing the posterior distribution. This mirrors what we saw in [Chapter 8](08-bayesian-inference-decision-theory.qmd#the-search-for-air-france-flight-447) with the search for Air France Flight 447, where Bayesian methods succeeded by combining multiple uncertain sources of information rather than relying on any single piece of evidence. Similarly, for LIGO, using Bayesian methods allowed them to say things like "we're 90% confident the total mass was between 60 and 70 solar masses" rather than just "the mass was 65 solar masses."

The LIGO team used specialized inference software developed over many years specifically for gravitational wave astronomy. But here's the key insight: the same fundamental challenges they faced -- combining uncertain information, quantifying confidence in conclusions, handling complex models that do not admit closed-form solutions -- appear everywhere in modern data science. 

While it used to be the case, it's not very efficient for every researcher to spend years building custom inference software for their specific problems. **That's where general-purpose probabilistic programming tools come in.** They let us tackle similarly complex problems whether we're:

- **Aggregating opinion polls** to predict election outcomes (like [The Economist's 2020 US presidential forecast](https://github.com/TheEconomist/us-potus-model), written in Stan by The Economist together with the statisticians Andrew Gelman and Merlin Heidemanns)
- **Analyzing genomic data** to identify bacterial strains in microbiome studies
- **Detecting anomalies** in sensor networks or financial transactions
- **Personalizing recommendations** while quantifying uncertainty

In all these problems, we need methods that can handle complex models with dozens or even thousands of parameters, incorporate prior knowledge, and -- crucially -- tell us how uncertain the results are. The promise of probabilistic programming tools is to allow the researcher to focus on defining the model, and the machinery should automatically take care of the inference.^[In practice, as we will see in this chapter, some work and expertise is still needed.]

### From Models to Inference

The gap between statistical theory and practice used to be enormous. Suppose you write down a Bayesian model on paper:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Prior: } & \theta \sim \pi(\theta) \\
\text{Likelihood: } & X \mid \theta \sim f(X \mid \theta) \\
\text{Posterior: } & \theta \mid X \sim \frac{f(X \mid \theta) \pi(\theta)}{\int f(X \mid \theta') \pi(\theta') d\theta'}
\end{aligned}$$

How do you solve this? Outside of a few special cases with conjugate priors (like Beta-Binomial or Normal-Normal from [Chapter 8](08-bayesian-inference-decision-theory.qmd)), we can't write down a formula for the posterior distribution.

So how do we actually do Bayesian inference in practice? One common solution is that **we don't compute the posterior distribution directly -- we draw samples from it.** Using algorithms like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), we generate hundreds or thousands of samples $\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_m$ that, collectively, represent the posterior distribution.

::: {.callout-note}
## Samples are Practical Representations of Distributions

Samples are a very convenient way of representing a probability distribution, whether discrete or continuous. Computing any statistic via samples is straightforward: 

- the mean is `np.mean(samples)`
- a 95% credible interval is `np.percentile(samples, [2.5, 97.5])`
- $p(\theta > 0)$ is just `np.mean(samples > 0)`
- the distribution of some complex function $g(\theta)$ is `g(samples)`

The tradeoff is clear: we lose analytical precision but gain computational flexibility.
:::

This is the key insight of practical Bayesian inference: we approximate the posterior through samples rather than computing it exactly. But implementing MCMC correctly used to require deep expertise -- you needed to code your own sampler, tune it properly, and verify convergence. If you got any of these steps wrong, your results would be unreliable.

## MCMC Foundations and Diagnostics

### MCMC Basics

As we mentioned earlier, practical Bayesian inference hinges on a simple but powerful idea: instead of computing integrals analytically (usually impossible), we draw samples from the posterior distribution and use those samples to approximate any quantity we need. 

We primarily obtain these samples using **Markov Chain Monte Carlo** (MCMC). MCMC is not a single algorithm, but a broad family of different methods that allow us to sample from a target probability distribution $\pi(\theta)$, provided its *unnormalized* density.

#### The Target: Unnormalized Posteriors

Our goal is to sample from the posterior distribution:

$$f(\theta \mid x^n) = \frac{f(x^n \mid \theta) f(\theta)}{\int f(x^n \mid \theta) f(\theta) d\theta}$$

The denominator -- the marginal likelihood -- is typically intractable. But here's the fundamental point: **MCMC methods only need the unnormalized posterior**:

$$f(\theta \mid x^n) \propto f(x^n \mid \theta) f(\theta)$$

This is exactly what we can compute easily! The likelihood times the prior gives us an unnormalized density that's proportional to the posterior, and we can feed this to MCMC algorithms.^[There are complex models for which computing the likelihood itself can be difficult, but for most models we consider this is not an issue.]

#### Markov Chains: From Local Moves to Global Exploration

MCMC algorithms iteratively construct a stochastic sequence -- a Markov chain $\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_m$ -- that explores the parameter space. In the chain, each new state depends only on the current state (the Markov property):

$$\theta_{m+1} \sim p(\theta_{m+1} | \theta_m)$$

where $p(\theta_{m+1} | \theta_m)$ depends on the *transition kernel* unique to each MCMC algorithm. Many algorithms use **local** transitions (close to the current point), but locality is not required and modern methods can efficiently explore far from the current point.
This probability distribution is designed following mathematical rules such that the stationary distribution of the produced chain is our target posterior.^[The exact way in which this is achieved depends on the specific choice of MCMC algorithm.]

Think of it like a guided random walk through parameter space: the algorithm is built in such a way that it tends to spend more time in high-probability regions (where the posterior is large) and less time in low-probability regions, producing samples that represent the posterior distribution.

In modern implementations, MCMC sampling is typically split into two phases:

1. **Warm-up/Adaptation** (also called burn-in): Starting from an arbitrary point, the algorithm moves toward higher-probability regions, often adjusting its own settings as it goes. We **discard** these early draws.^[Adjusting sampling settings throughout this phase breaks the Markov property: to collect valid samples, adaptation must be stopped before collecting valid draws, and we throw away the burn-in samples.]
2. **Sampling**: With settings fixed, the chain explores the high-probability region and we keep these draws as our posterior samples.

::: {.callout-warning}
## MCMC Samples Are Correlated

Consecutive MCMC draws are **not independent** because each step builds on the previous one. This is normal and does not invalidate the correctness of the algorithm, but it means you may need more iterations to get a good picture of the posterior. Use standard diagnostics (built into most libraries) to detect if you have collected enough samples and, if needed, run longer or use more efficient samplers. Even advanced methods still show some correlation for challenging problems.
:::

### Convergence and $\hat{R}$ {#sec-convergence-rhat}

MCMC algorithms are constructed such that they are **mathematically guaranteed** to **converge in distribution** to the target ([Chapter 3](03-convergence-inference.qmd)): as $m \to \infty$, the distribution of $\theta_m$ approaches $\pi(\theta)$.^[The guarantee holds under mild conditions on the chain: it must be able to reach every region where $\pi(\theta) > 0$, and it must not cycle periodically. MCMC algorithms are designed to meet these conditions.] However, this guarantee comes with two critical caveats:

1. **We can only run for finite time**: While convergence is guaranteed asymptotically, in practice we must stop after a finite number of iterations.
2. **No finite-time proof**: For most MCMC algorithms, it's impossible to prove that convergence has been achieved after any specific number of iterations.

This creates a fundamental challenge: How do we know if our MCMC sampler has converged to the correct distribution? While we can never be completely certain, we can detect when it almost certainly *has not* converged by running multiple chains and checking whether they're all exploring the same distribution. The most common diagnostic for this is the $\hat{R}$ statistic.

::: {.tabbed-content}
## Intuitive

MCMC tries to generate samples from an unknown probability distribution (the posterior). Since we can't sample directly, we use a clever trick: we create a random walk that naturally spends more time in high-probability regions and less time in low-probability regions.

**But how do we know our random walk has found the right distribution?** We run multiple independent walks (chains) starting from different places and check if they all end up with the same behavior:

- All chains visit the same regions with the same frequency → No sign of problems ✓
- Different chains are stuck in different regions or show different patterns → Not converged ✗  
- Some chains are still moving away from their starting points → Still warming up ✗

The key insight: agreement between independent chains is only moderate evidence that sampling went well, since all the chains can miss the same region of the posterior. Disagreement is a clear sign we need to run longer or fix our sampler.

## Mathematical

The most common convergence diagnostic is the $\hat{R}$ statistic (Potential Scale Reduction Factor), which quantifies whether independent chains have converged to the same distribution.

Conceptually, $\hat{R}$ can be understood as:

$$\hat{R} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\text{Between-chain variance} + \text{Within-chain variance}}{\text{Within-chain variance}}}$$

This is a simplification -- modern software computes rank-normalized split-$\hat{R}$ [@vehtari2021rank], which splits chains, applies rank normalization for robustness, and uses more sophisticated variance estimators. Use library implementations rather than coding this yourself.

For well-converged chains:

- Between-chain variance ≈ Within-chain variance
- Therefore $\hat{R} \approx 1.0$
- **Practical threshold**: $\hat{R} \leq 1.01$ indicates convergence

Values above 1.01 suggest the chains haven't fully mixed and are exploring different regions of the parameter space.

```{python}
# Good convergence: all chains explore the same distribution
# Simulate AR(1) process to mimic realistic MCMC autocorrelation
rho = 0.9  # autocorrelation coefficient (higher = more correlation)
for i in range(4):
    chain = np.zeros(n_samples)
    chain[0] = np.random.randn() * 0.5  # Start near the target
    for t in range(1, n_samples):
        chain[t] = rho * chain[t-1] + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * np.random.randn()
    ax1.plot(chain, alpha=0.7, linewidth=0.8)
ax1.set_title("Good Convergence (R̂ ≈ 1.00)")
ax1.set_xlabel("Iteration")
ax1.set_ylabel("Parameter Value")

# Poor convergence: chains stuck in different regions
for i in range(4):
    chain = np.zeros(n_samples)
    # Each chain gets stuck around a different mean
    chain_mean = np.random.uniform(-3, 3)
    chain[0] = chain_mean + np.random.randn() * 0.5
    
    # High autocorrelation but also drift toward different means
    rho = 0.98  # High autocorrelation
    for t in range(1, n_samples):
        # AR process that stays near its own mean
        chain[t] = chain_mean + rho * (chain[t-1] - chain_mean) + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * np.random.randn() * 0.8
    ax2.plot(chain, alpha=0.7, linewidth=0.8)
ax2.set_title("Poor Convergence (R̂ > 1.01)")
ax2.set_xlabel("Iteration")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The left panel shows an example of **converged chains**: all four chains quickly found the target distribution and are exploring the same region, creating overlapping "fuzzy caterpillar" patterns. Each chain visits similar values with similar frequency - this is what we want to see.

The right panel shows an example of **non-converged chains**: they started far apart and haven't yet found a common distribution. Some chains are stuck in different regions (perhaps different modes), while others are still wandering. These samples are not representative of the true posterior - using them for inference would be misleading!

**The key insight:** Good convergence shows all chains "agreeing" about the posterior distribution, exploring the same regions with the same frequency.

## Hello, PyMC: Building and Sampling a Simple Model

### Defining Probabilistic Models: Joint → Conditional

Let's understand how probabilistic programming languages represent models. Any joint distribution can be factorized using the chain rule:

$$f(y_1, \dots, y_n) = f(y_1 \mid y_2, \dots, y_n) \cdot f(y_2 \mid y_3, \dots, y_n) \cdots f(y_n)$$

This factorization works for any ordering of variables. In practice, we choose an ordering that makes the conditional distributions simple to specify.

For a basic Bayesian model with parameters $\theta$ and data $X$, the simplest factorization is:

$$f(\theta, X) = f(X \mid \theta) \cdot f(\theta)$$

This is the familiar likelihood times prior, which we can express using sampling notation:

$$\begin{aligned}
\theta &\sim f(\theta) && \text{(prior)} \\
X \mid \theta &\sim f(X \mid \theta) && \text{(likelihood)}
\end{aligned}$$

In probabilistic programming, as in Bayesian statistics, there is no fundamental distinction between parameters and data: both are random variables that we represent using this sampling notation. The only difference is that data values are observed (fixed to measured values), while parameter values are unobserved (to be inferred).

::: {.callout-tip icon=false}
## Example: Measuring Heights with Unknown Mean and Variance

Consider measuring heights in a population where we assume both mean and variance are independent and unknown. Based on our prior over the parameters, our generative model is:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mu &\sim \text{Normal}(170, 20) && \text{(mean height in cm)} \\
\sigma &\sim \text{HalfNormal}(10) && \text{(standard deviation in cm)} \\
\text{heights} \mid \mu, \sigma &\sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma) && \text{(individual heights in cm)}
\end{aligned}$$

The joint distribution is:
$$f(\mu, \sigma, \text{heights}) = f(\text{heights} \mid \mu, \sigma) \cdot f(\sigma) \cdot f(\mu)$$

This translates to code as:

::: {.tabbed-content}
## PyMC

```python
mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=170, sigma=20)
sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=10)
heights = pm.Normal("heights", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=data)
```

## Stan

```stan

  real<lower=0> sigma;    // Constrained to be positive
}

  sigma ~ normal(0, 10);    // Truncated at 0 due to constraint
  heights ~ normal(mu, sigma);  
}
```
:::
:::

The power of probabilistic programming is that this same approach scales to much more complex models -- hierarchical structures, time series, mixture models -- all using the same basic principle: we describe the generative process, and the system handles the inference.

## Choosing Priors: Art Meets Science

Our prior $\text{Normal}(210, 40)$ reflects medical knowledge that total cholesterol typically ranges from 130-290 mg/dL in adults. ## What's Actually Happening During Sampling?

Modern probabilistic programming uses an algorithm called NUTS (No-U-Turn Sampler) that automatically explores the posterior distribution. Two key features:

**Multiple chains**: PyMC runs multiple independent chains (typically 4), each starting from a different random initial value. This parallelizes computation (using multiple CPU cores) and is essential for convergence diagnostics - we need multiple chains to verify they all find the same posterior distribution. In our example, we're using the default number of chains (likely 4 on most systems).

**Two-phase sampling**: Each chain goes through:

1. **Tuning/Warmup**: The sampler learns the "shape" of your posterior -- where to look, how big steps to take. These samples are discarded. PyMC's default is typically 1000 iterations, but we use `tune=500` here for speed.
2. **Sampling**: Using what it learned, the sampler collects the actual samples we'll use for inference. Only these post-warmup samples are returned and used for all subsequent analysis. PyMC's default is typically 1000 draws, but we use `draws=500` here for speed.

With our settings (500 post-warmup draws per chain, likely 4 chains), we get 2000 total posterior samples. The iteration counts aren't rules -- complex models might need thousands, simple ones might need fewer. The key innovation is that NUTS adapts automatically, unlike older methods that required manual tuning for each problem.
:::

#### Step 5: Convergence Diagnostics

Before trusting our posterior samples, we must check for signs that the MCMC sampler has not converged. Recall from [the discussion of convergence and $\hat{R}$](#sec-convergence-rhat) that convergence means our chains have found and are exploring the target distribution.

All diagnostics below are computed using only the post-warmup samples -- the warmup/tuning iterations have already been discarded. With our settings (4 chains, 500 samples each), we're analyzing 2000 posterior draws total.

##### Numerical Diagnostics

First, let's check the key convergence statistics. Modern MCMC diagnostics focus on two critical measures:

**$\hat{R}$ (R-hat)**: Compares variation between chains to variation within chains. Values close to 1.0 mean that the chains agree with each other. The threshold $\hat{R} \leq 1.01$ has become standard based on extensive empirical studies [@vehtari2021rank] showing it reliably detects non-convergence.

**ESS (Effective Sample Size)**: Because MCMC samples are autocorrelated, 1000 samples don't provide as much information as 1000 independent draws. ESS estimates the equivalent number of independent samples. The conventional threshold of ESS > 400 total (or 100 per chain with 4 chains) typically ensures stable estimates for posterior means and 95% credible intervals, though more extreme quantiles may require higher ESS.^[The ESS > 400 guideline is a practical recommendation from the probabilistic programming community [@stan2024reference; @gelman2013bayesian] rather than a hard theoretical requirement. For posterior means, ESS of 100-200 might suffice, while extreme tail probabilities (e.g., 99th percentiles) may require ESS > 1000. The 400 threshold represents a reasonable default for common use cases like computing 95% credible intervals.]

## When Diagnostics Fail

If you see:

- **$\hat{R} > 1.01$**: Chains haven't converged to the same distribution
- **Low ESS** (< 100 per chain): Too much autocorrelation in your samples
- **Trends in trace plots**: Chains still moving toward the target distribution
- **Multimodal traces**: Chains stuck exploring different regions

When diagnostics indicate problems, the most common solution is simply to run the sampler longer (more warmup and/or more iterations). If problems persist, it usually indicates issues with the model specification that require more careful investigation.

## Example: The A/B Test From Chapter 7, the Bayesian Way

The introduction of [Chapter 7](07-hypothesis-testing.qmd#introduction-is-an-observed-effect-real-or-just-random-chance) describes an online retailer's A/B test: 850 of the 1000 users shown a new interface made a purchase, against 800 of the 1000 users shown the old one. Chapter 7 asks whether the difference is statistically significant. The Bayesian question is more direct: given the data, how probable is it that the new interface has the higher purchase rate?

Write a PyMC model with purchase rates $p_{\text{new}}$ and $p_{\text{old}}$, independent $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ priors, and a Binomial likelihood for each group's number of purchases. Then:

1. Sample from the posterior and check convergence.
2. Compute the posterior mean and a 95% credible interval for the difference $\delta = p_{\text{new}} - p_{\text{old}}$.
3. Compute the posterior probability $\mathbb{P}(p_{\text{new}} > p_{\text{old}} \mid \text{data})$.
4. The Beta prior is conjugate to this likelihood ([Chapter 8](08-bayesian-inference-decision-theory.qmd#example-the-bernoulli-beta-model)). Use the exact posterior to check the MCMC posterior means.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
## Solution

`pm.Binomial` takes the number of trials `n` and the success probability `p`, and `observed=` supplies the observed number of successes:

```{python}
#| echo: true

with pm.Model() as ab_model:
    # Priors: uniform on each purchase rate
    p_new = pm.Beta("p_new", alpha=1, beta=1)
    p_old = pm.Beta("p_old", alpha=1, beta=1)

    # Likelihood: purchases out of 1000 users in each group
    purchases_new = pm.Binomial("purchases_new", n=1000, p=p_new, observed=850)
    purchases_old = pm.Binomial("purchases_old", n=1000, p=p_old, observed=800)

    idata_ab = pm.sample(draws=1000, tune=1000, random_seed=42, progressbar=False)

print(az.summary(idata_ab, var_names=["p_new", "p_old"]))
```

Both $\hat{R}$ values are 1.0 and the effective sample sizes are well above 400, so the diagnostics show no sign of problems.

The model has no variable for $\delta$, and it does not need one. Each posterior draw is a pair of values $(p_{\text{new}}, p_{\text{old}})$, so subtracting them draw by draw gives samples of $\delta$, as described in the callout *Samples are Practical Representations of Distributions*:

### The Dirichlet-Categorical Model

When we have categorical data, we need a different statistical model. The natural choice is:

- **Data**: Each observation belongs to one of $K$ categories
- **Parameter**: A probability vector $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, \ldots, p_K)$ where $p_k$ is the probability of category $k$
- **Constraint**: The probabilities must sum to 1: $\sum_{k=1}^K p_k = 1$
- **Likelihood**: Each observation is a draw from the Categorical distribution, $X_i \mid \mathbf{p} \sim \text{Categorical}(\mathbf{p})$, meaning $\mathbb{P}(X_i = k) = p_k$

This constraint means $\mathbf{p}$ lives on the **probability simplex** -- the set of all valid probability distributions over $K$ categories. For our 3-category cholesterol data, this is a triangular surface in 3D space where any point represents a valid probability distribution. Since the three coordinates always sum to 1, we can draw the triangle flat, as in the figure below. Each corner is the distribution that puts all its probability on one category, the centre is $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$, and a point gives a category more probability the further it lies from the edge opposite that category's corner.

The [Dirichlet distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_distribution) is the natural prior for probability vectors:

$$\mathbf{p} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_K), \quad \alpha_k > 0$$

It extends the Beta distribution of [Chapter 8](08-bayesian-inference-decision-theory.qmd#example-the-bernoulli-beta-model) from two categories to $K$. With $K = 2$ the probability vector is $(p, 1 - p)$, and $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ is the same as $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$. The interpretation carries over too: just as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in the Beta act as **pseudo-counts** of prior successes and failures, $\alpha_k$ acts as a pseudo-count of prior observations in category $k$.

The parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ does two separate jobs, one through its ratios and one through its sum. Write $\alpha_0 = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k$ for the sum:

- **The ratios set the mean**: $\mathbb{E}[p_k] = \alpha_k / \alpha_0$. For example, $\text{Dirichlet}(2, 4, 8)$ and $\text{Dirichlet}(1, 2, 4)$ have the same mean, $(1/7, 2/7, 4/7)$.
- **The sum sets the concentration**: $\alpha_0$ is the total number of pseudo-observations, and the larger it is, the more tightly $\mathbf{p}$ clusters around its mean. Writing $m_k = \alpha_k/\alpha_0$ for the mean of $p_k$, its variance is $\mathbb{V}(p_k) = m_k(1 - m_k)/(\alpha_0 + 1)$.

Two special cases are worth knowing. With all $\alpha_k = 1$ the Dirichlet is **uniform** over the simplex: every probability vector is equally likely. With all $\alpha_k < 1$ it favours **sparse** vectors, pushing its mass towards the edges and corners of the simplex, where one or two categories take nearly all the probability.

```{python}
#| echo: true
#| code-fold: true
#| code-summary: "Show plotting code"
#| fig-width: 7
#| fig-height: 2.2

def simplex_xy(p):
    """Map probability vectors (rows of p) to 2D points in the triangle."""
    p = np.atleast_2d(p)
    return p[:, 1] + 0.5 * p[:, 2], (np.sqrt(3) / 2) * p[:, 2]

def draw_simplex(ax, labels, fontsize=8):
    """Draw the triangle with a label at each corner."""
    h = np.sqrt(3) / 2
    ax.plot([0, 1, 0.5, 0], [0, 0, h, 0], color='black', linewidth=0.8)
    ax.text(-0.03, -0.05, labels[0], ha='right', va='top', fontsize=fontsize)
    ax.text(1.03, -0.05, labels[1], ha='left', va='top', fontsize=fontsize)
    ax.text(0.5, h + 0.04, labels[2], ha='center', va='bottom', fontsize=fontsize)
    ax.set_aspect('equal')
    ax.set_xlim(-0.3, 1.3)
    ax.set_ylim(-0.15, 1.0)
    ax.axis('off')

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
alphas = [(1, 1, 1), (10, 10, 10), (2, 4, 8), (0.3, 0.3, 0.3)]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(7, 2.2))
for ax, alpha in zip(axes, alphas):
    draws = rng.dirichlet(alpha, size=1000)
    x, y_pos = simplex_xy(draws)
    draw_simplex(ax, ['$p_1$', '$p_2$', '$p_3$'])
    ax.scatter(x, y_pos, s=2, alpha=0.3, color='C0', linewidths=0)
    mean_x, mean_y = simplex_xy(np.array(alpha) / np.sum(alpha))
    ax.plot(mean_x, mean_y, 'x', color='red', markersize=8, markeredgewidth=2)
    ax.set_title(f"Dirichlet{alpha}".replace(" ", ""), fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Each blue dot is one probability vector drawn from the Dirichlet, and the red cross marks its mean. $\text{Dirichlet}(1, 1, 1)$ spreads evenly over the whole triangle. $\text{Dirichlet}(10, 10, 10)$ has the same mean, $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$, but 30 pseudo-observations instead of 3, so its draws cluster near the centre. $\text{Dirichlet}(2, 4, 8)$ moves the mean towards the $p_3$ corner. $\text{Dirichlet}(0.3, 0.3, 0.3)$ piles its draws along the edges and into the corners.

## Dirichlet-Categorical Conjugacy

The Dirichlet and Categorical form a conjugate pair, just like the Beta and Bernoulli:

- **Prior**: $\mathbf{p} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$
- **Likelihood**: Observe $n_k$ instances of category $k$, out of $n = \sum_k n_k$ observations
- **Posterior**: $\mathbf{p} \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{n})$, where $\mathbf{n} = (n_1, \ldots, n_K)$

The posterior simply adds the observed counts to the prior pseudo-counts! Its mean, $(\alpha_k + n_k)/(\alpha_0 + n)$, is a compromise between the prior mean $\alpha_k/\alpha_0$ and the observed proportion $n_k/n$, exactly as for the Beta.
:::

## Prior Sensitivity: How Much Does the Prior Matter?

## Bayesian Model Comparison

So far we have fitted one model at a time, but often there are several candidate models for the same data and we need to choose between them. Here is a concrete question from the Framingham data: **do women and men differ in their mean cholesterol?** Each possible answer is a model:

- **Model 1 (pooled)**: everyone shares one mean, $X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma)$.
- **Model 2 (by sex)**: women and men each have their own mean, $X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_{s_i}, \sigma)$, where $s_i \in \{\text{female}, \text{male}\}$ is the sex of subject $i$.

Model 1 is the special case of Model 2 with $\mu_{\text{female}} = \mu_{\text{male}}$, so choosing between them is the Bayesian counterpart of the two-sample test in [Chapter 7](07-hypothesis-testing.qmd) (*Example: Comparing Two Means*). Model 2 is also a regression of cholesterol on a single binary predictor, as in [Chapter 9](09-linear-logistic-regression.qmd).

In a Bayesian context, testing is usually performed via **model comparison**. The aim is to infer which of multiple models $\mathcal{M}_1$, $\ldots$, $\mathcal{M}_k$ is the best fit for the data. What "best" means depends on what we are willing to assume about reality.

### Two Philosophies of Model Comparison

Bayesian model comparison comes in two fundamentally different flavors, depending on what we believe about reality [@kelter2021bayesian]:

**$\mathcal{M}$-closed perspective**: Assumes one of our candidate models is actually the true data-generating process. The set $\mathcal{M}$ of models is "closed" because it contains the truth -- we just need to figure out which one it is. This classical Bayesian approach treats model selection like a multiple choice question where one answer is definitely correct. We compute posterior probabilities over models, $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i \mid X^n = x^n)$, asking: "Given the data, what's the probability that Model $i$ is the true model?"

**$\mathcal{M}$-open perspective**: Acknowledges that all our models are wrong -- approximations of a reality that's more complex than anything we can write down. The model set is "open" because the true process lies outside it. Since no model is true, we can't ask "which one is correct?" Instead, we ask "which approximation makes the best predictions?" This is the more realistic perspective for most real-world problems.

In practice, the $\mathcal{M}$-open perspective is more realistic. Statistician [George E. P. Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_E._P._Box) famously said "All models are wrong, but some are useful." Our two models are clearly simplifications: cholesterol is not exactly normally distributed (its distribution is skewed to the right), and many factors besides sex affect it. So we'll focus on predictive performance.

### Model Comparison in the $\mathcal{M}$-Closed Setting

In the $\mathcal{M}$-closed approach, we apply Bayes' theorem directly to models:

$$\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i \mid X^n = x^n) \propto f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_i) \cdot \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_i)$$

The key quantity here is $f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_i)$, called the **marginal likelihood** or **evidence**, which integrates the likelihood over all possible parameter values weighted by the prior:

$$f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_i) = \int f(x^n \mid \theta, \mathcal{M}_i) f(\theta \mid \mathcal{M}_i) \, d\theta$$

This integral averages how well the model explains the data across all parameter values the prior considers plausible.

To compare two models, we often compute the ratio of their posterior probabilities:

$$\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_1 \mid x^n)}{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_2 \mid x^n)} = \frac{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_2)} \cdot \frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_1)}{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_2)}$$

When we assign equal prior probabilities to both models (no preference a priori), this simplifies to just the ratio of marginal likelihoods, called the **Bayes factor**:

$$\text{BF}_{12} = \frac{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{f(x^n \mid \mathcal{M}_2)}$$

A Bayes factor of 10 means the data are 10 times more likely under Model 1 than Model 2.

::: {.callout-note}
## A Note on Prior Sensitivity

Bayes factors are widely used and mathematically principled, but they do have an important limitation: **they are sensitive to the prior**, especially its width.

Making a prior twice as wide can dramatically reduce the marginal likelihood, even if the posterior barely changes. This happens because the likelihood is usually much more concentrated than the prior. A broader prior "wastes" probability mass on unlikely parameter values, reducing the evidence. In our example, widening the priors on the means would penalise Model 2, which has two of them, more than Model 1, and so shift the Bayes factor towards Model 1 without changing either posterior much.

This sensitivity doesn't make Bayes factors wrong -- they correctly answer the question "which model is more probable?" under the $\mathcal{M}$-closed assumption. But it does mean you need confidence in your prior specification, which can be challenging in practice.
:::

### The Alternative: Predictive Model Comparison

Given this sensitivity and the fact that all our models are approximations anyway, this chapter focuses on the $\mathcal{M}$-open approach. Instead of asking "which model is true?" (when none are), we ask "which model makes better predictions?" This shifts our focus from model probability to predictive performance. It is the same principle as model selection in [Chapter 9](09-linear-logistic-regression.qmd): judge a model by how well it predicts new data, not by how well it fits the data it was trained on.

#### Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD)

To predict a new observation $x^\star$ after seeing data $X^n$, we use the **posterior predictive distribution**:

$$p(x^\star \mid X^n) = \int p(x^\star \mid \theta) p(\theta \mid X^n) \, d\theta$$

Taking the logarithm gives the **log predictive density (LPD)** for that specific observation:

$$\text{LPD}(x^\star) = \log p(x^\star \mid X^n)$$

But LPD for a single point isn't enough -- it depends on which $x^\star$ we happen to see. We need the **expected log predictive density (ELPD)** over all possible future observations:

$$\text{ELPD} = \mathbb{E}_{x^\star \sim p_{\text{true}}}[\text{LPD}(x^\star)]$$

This measures average predictive performance, which is what we actually care about for model comparison.

In practice with MCMC samples $\theta_i \sim p(\theta \mid X^n)$, we approximate LPD as:

$$\widehat{\text{LPD}}(x^\star) = \log \left( \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m p(x^\star \mid \theta_i) \right)$$

#### Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation for ELPD

We can't compute true ELPD without knowing $p_{\text{true}}$ and having infinite future data. Scoring the model on the data it was fitted to would be too optimistic, just as training error is in Chapter 9. The solution is **leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV)**, which Chapter 9 introduced with squared prediction error as the score: treat each existing observation as a "future" test point for a model trained without it. Here, each held-out point is scored by its log predictive density instead.

For each observation $x_k$, we'd ideally:

1. Obtain the posterior $p(\theta \mid X_{-k})$ using all data except $x_k$
2. Compute the posterior predictive $p(x_k \mid X_{-k}) = \int p(x_k \mid \theta) p(\theta \mid X_{-k}) d\theta$
3. Sum over all $k$ to estimate ELPD

This gives **ELPD-LOO**:

$$\text{ELPD-LOO} = \sum_{k=1}^n \log p(x_k \mid X_{-k})$$

Actually refitting the model $n$ times would be computationally prohibitive. Fortunately, we can approximate this efficiently by reweighting our existing posterior samples:

$$\text{ELPD-LOO} = \sum_{k=1}^n \log \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^m w_i^{(k)} p(x_k \mid \theta_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^m w_i^{(k)}} \right)$$

where the weights $w_i^{(k)}$ are computed using **Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS)**. This is what ArviZ's `loo()` function implements. It needs the pointwise log-likelihoods $\log p(x_k \mid \theta_i)$ for every observation $x_k$ and posterior draw $\theta_i$, which PyMC stores only when asked, by calling `pm.sample()` with `idata_kwargs={"log_likelihood": True}`.

### Does Cholesterol Differ by Sex?

To fit the two models we need priors. For each mean ($\mu$ in Model 1, $\mu_{\text{female}}$ and $\mu_{\text{male}}$ in Model 2) we reuse the $\text{Normal}(210, 40)$ prior of the Normal-Normal model. Unlike in that model, $\sigma$ is now unknown, and we give it a $\text{HalfNormal}(50)$ prior, as in *Example: Measuring Heights with Unknown Mean and Variance*. These priors are not conjugate to the likelihood, so this time there is no exact posterior to check MCMC against.

We start with the same 30 subjects as before:

```{python}
#| echo: true

# Sex of the same 30 subjects (CHOL has no missing values, so rows line up)
sex = fram_data['SEX'].values[sample_indices]
# Index 0 for women, 1 for men: Model 2 uses it to pick each subject's mean
sex_idx = (sex == "male").astype(int)

print(f"Our sample: {np.sum(sex_idx == 0)} women, {np.sum(sex_idx == 1)} men")
print(f"Sample mean for women: {chol_sample[sex_idx == 0].mean():.1f} mg/dL")
print(f"Sample mean for men:   {chol_sample[sex_idx == 1].mean():.1f} mg/dL")
```

We write each model as a function of the data, so that we can refit it to the full cohort later:

```{python}
#| echo: true

def fit_pooled(chol):
    """Model 1: one mean for everyone."""
    with pm.Model():
        # Priors
        mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=210, sigma=40)
        sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=50)
        # Likelihood
        pm.Normal("chol", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=chol)
        # Store pointwise log-likelihoods: LOO needs them
        return pm.sample(draws=1000, tune=1000, random_seed=42, progressbar=False,
                         idata_kwargs={"log_likelihood": True})

def fit_by_sex(chol, sex_idx):
    """Model 2: one mean per sex."""
    with pm.Model():
        # Priors: mu[0] is the mean for women, mu[1] the mean for men
        mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=210, sigma=40, shape=2)
        sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", sigma=50)
        # Likelihood: mu[sex_idx] gives each subject the mean of their group
        pm.Normal("chol", mu=mu[sex_idx], sigma=sigma, observed=chol)
        return pm.sample(draws=1000, tune=1000, random_seed=42, progressbar=False,
                         idata_kwargs={"log_likelihood": True})

idata_pooled_30 = fit_pooled(chol_sample)
idata_sex_30 = fit_by_sex(chol_sample, sex_idx)

print(az.summary(idata_pooled_30))
print(az.summary(idata_sex_30))
```

The argument `shape=2` makes `mu` a vector of two means, and indexing it with `sex_idx` plays the role of the indicator variable in a regression with a binary predictor. The diagnostics show no sign of problems for either model: every $\hat{R}$ is 1.0 and the effective sample sizes are well above 400.

Now we compare them with `az.compare()`, which computes ELPD-LOO for each model and ranks them:

```{python}
#| echo: true

comparison_30 = az.compare({"Pooled": idata_pooled_30, "By sex": idata_sex_30})
print(comparison_30)
```

The comparison table shows models ranked by ELPD-LOO (higher is better). The key columns are:

- **rank**: from best (0) to worst
- **elpd_loo**: the ELPD-LOO estimate for each model
- **p_loo**: the effective number of parameters, an estimate of how flexible the model is
- **elpd_diff**: the difference in ELPD-LOO from the best model (0 for the best model, positive for the others)
- **dse**: the standard error of that **difference**
- **se**: the standard error of each model's individual ELPD-LOO (not used for comparison!)

::: {.callout-warning collapse="true"}
## Important: Don't Compare Individual Standard Errors!

The `se` column shows each model's individual ELPD uncertainty. You **cannot** determine if models are significantly different by checking if the intervals $\text{elpd\_loo} \pm 2\,\text{se}$ overlap - that would be statistically incorrect.

Why? Both models are fitted to the same data, so their ELPD estimates are correlated (they make similar errors on the same hard-to-predict observations). The proper comparison uses the standard error of the **difference**, `dse`, which `az.compare()` computes for us.
:::

To decide whether a difference is meaningful, we use the Wald test of [Chapter 7](07-hypothesis-testing.qmd). Treating `elpd_diff` as approximately Normal with standard error `dse`, the difference is significant at the 5% level if the interval $\text{elpd\_diff} \pm 2\,\text{dse}$ does not include 0, that is, if $\text{elpd\_diff} > 2\,\text{dse}$.^[The normal approximation is rough when the difference is small. Aki Vehtari's [cross-validation FAQ](https://users.aalto.fi/~ave/CV-FAQ.html) recommends reading an `elpd_diff` below about 4 as a small difference, whatever its `dse`.]

With 30 subjects, the pooled model ranks first, and the by-sex model's `elpd_diff` is below $2\,\text{dse}$: the data cannot tell the two models apart. The women in our sample average about 13 mg/dL more than the men, but with a standard deviation of around 60 mg/dL in this sample, a gap of that size easily arises by chance in groups of 18 and 12 people. The `p_loo` column shows the price of Model 2's extra mean. An extra parameter raises ELPD-LOO only if it improves the predictions for held-out points by more than the extra estimation noise costs, and here it does not. AIC makes the same trade-off explicit in [Chapter 9](09-linear-logistic-regression.qmd): on the log-likelihood scale of ELPD-LOO, its penalty is one unit per parameter, and `p_loo` plays the role of the parameter count.

Failing to find a difference is not the same as showing there is none, and Model 2 is not rejected either. Let's refit both models to the full cohort:

```{python}
#| echo: true

chol_all = fram_data['CHOL'].values
sex_idx_all = (fram_data['SEX'].values == "male").astype(int)
print(f"Full cohort: {np.sum(sex_idx_all == 0)} women, {np.sum(sex_idx_all == 1)} men")

idata_pooled_all = fit_pooled(chol_all)
idata_sex_all = fit_by_sex(chol_all, sex_idx_all)

comparison_all = az.compare({"Pooled": idata_pooled_all, "By sex": idata_sex_all})
print(comparison_all)
```

Now the by-sex model ranks first, and the pooled model's `elpd_diff` is more than three times its `dse`, a clear difference by the $2\,\text{dse}$ rule. The figure shows both comparisons as the difference in ELPD-LOO between Model 2 and Model 1, with error bars of $\pm 2\,\text{dse}$:

```{python}
#| echo: true
#| fig-width: 7
#| fig-height: 2.2

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(7, 2.2))
panels = [(f"Our sample (n = {sample_size})", comparison_30),
          (f"Full cohort (n = {len(chol_all)})", comparison_all)]
for ax, (title, comp) in zip(axes, panels):
    diff = comp.loc["By sex", "elpd_loo"] - comp.loc["Pooled", "elpd_loo"]
    dse = comp["dse"].iloc[1]  # The lower-ranked model's row holds the SE of the difference
    ax.errorbar(diff, 0, xerr=2 * dse, fmt='o', markersize=8, capsize=5,
                color='steelblue', ecolor='black', elinewidth=2)
    ax.axvline(0, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=1.5)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

**Key findings:**

1. **With 30 subjects, no difference is detectable.** The error bar straddles zero: the simpler pooled model ranks first, but neither model is rejected.
2. **With 1394 subjects, the by-sex model predicts clearly better.** The whole error bar lies to the right of zero.
3. **The models are the same in both cases; only the amount of data changed.** An extra parameter earns its place once there are enough data to estimate it well.

Model comparison tells us that giving each sex its own mean improves predictions. How large is the difference? The posterior samples of Model 2 answer that directly, as the draw-by-draw difference of the two means:

```{python}

mu_samples = idata_sex_all.posterior["mu"].values.reshape(-1, 2)

print(f"Posterior mean of mu_female - mu_male: {np.mean(diff_mu):.1f} mg/dL")
```

## Advanced Topic: Hierarchical Models

While beyond this chapter's scope, hierarchical models deserve mention as they're ubiquitous in modern Bayesian practice. These models have parameters that themselves follow probability distributions with hyperparameters:

$$\begin{aligned}
y_i &\sim f(y_i \mid \theta_i) \\
\theta_i &\sim g(\theta_i \mid \phi) \\
\phi &\sim h(\phi)
\end{aligned}$$

This creates "partial pooling" -- groups share information through the common hyperparameters $\phi$, but maintain their own parameters $\theta_i$. Examples include:

- Clinical trials with patient-specific effects
- Educational assessments across schools
- Time series with varying parameters

For comprehensive treatment, see Gelman et al.'s *Bayesian Data Analysis* or the Stan manual's case studies.

### Self-Test Problems

1. **Interpreting Convergence Diagnostics**: You run a model and see:
   ```
          mean    sd  hdi_3%  hdi_97%  r_hat  ess_bulk
   theta  5.23  8.91   -12.3    22.7    1.43      47
   ```
   What's wrong and how would you fix it?

   ::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
   ## Solution
   $\hat{R} = 1.43 >> 1.01$ means chains haven't converged. ESS = 47 << 400 means too few effective samples. Try: longer chains (`tune=2000`), higher `target_accept` (0.95), or better priors to avoid flat regions.
   :::

2. **Prior vs Data Influence**: Your prior is $\mu \sim \text{Normal}(210, 40)$ for cholesterol. You observe $\bar{x} = 230$ from $n=30$ samples with known $\sigma=35$. Will the posterior mean be closer to 210 or 230? Why?

   ::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
   ## Solution
   Compare precisions: prior precision $\tau_0^{-2} = 1/40^2 \approx 0.000625$, data precision $n/\sigma^2 = 30/35^2 \approx 0.0245$. Since data precision >> prior precision, the posterior will be pulled much closer to $\bar{x} = 230$.
   :::

3. **Dirichlet-Categorical Conjugacy**: You have a Dirichlet(1, 1, 1) prior for three categories. You observe counts (10, 15, 5). What's the posterior distribution?

   ::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
   ## Solution
   Dirichlet-Categorical conjugacy means you just add the counts to the prior parameters: posterior is Dirichlet(1+10, 1+15, 1+5) = Dirichlet(11, 16, 6). The posterior mean for category $k$ is $\alpha_k / \sum \alpha_i = 11/33$, $16/33$, $6/33$.
   :::

4. **Interpreting ELPD-LOO**: `az.compare()` ranks Model A first, and Model B's row shows elpd_diff = 3.5 with dse = 1.8. Is the difference meaningful?

   ::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
   ## Solution
   The difference is 3.5 in favor of Model A, with dse (standard error of the difference) = 1.8. Since 3.5 < 2×1.8 = 3.6, the difference is not statistically significant. The models perform similarly; neither is clearly better. Note: Use `dse` (difference SE), not `se` (individual model SE)!
   :::
